## Pre-event planning (the part phone apps cannot skip)

### Choose a format that fits the room

Common amateur choices:

- Single elimination for speed
- Short races for bar boxes and loud nights
- Slightly longer races for quieter rooms or stronger fields
- Clear rules for breaks, coaching, and timeouts

Do not invent complexity you cannot staff. A clean single-elimination race-to-two with explicit rules beats an ambitious format that stalls at midnight.

### Publish terms before registration

Players should know:

- Game and race
- Entry expectations (whatever your hall uses)
- Start time and check-in cutoff
- Table assignment method
- How disputes are resolved
- Whether the event is social-casual or competitive-serious

Ambiguity at signup becomes conflict at hill-hill.

### Align with the venue

Confirm table count, league conflicts, and whether a spectator display is welcome. Use **[/halls](/halls)** so players can find the room and check in as they arrive. Directors who ignore venue constraints create ghost fields—registered names, empty chairs.

## Running the event on RackUp: a floor playbook

### 1) Create the tournament

From **[/tournaments](/tournaments)**, create the event with the details you already decided. Name it like a human will search for it later (“Tuesday 9-Ball — Eastside Room”) instead of “test1.”

### 2) Open registration

Let players register in-app where possible. For walk-ups, have a director or desk helper add them before you start. Registration chaos is usually a cutoff problem, not a software problem—announce a hard close time and keep it.

### 3) Start when the field is real

Starting too early freezes out late arrivals; starting too late melts the room’s patience. Count bodies, not vibes. When you start, the bracket becomes the source of truth—treat it that way.

### 4) Report matches immediately

The biggest operational upgrade phones provide is **fast reporting**. Winning players (or designated table refs) should report as soon as the match ends. Delayed reporting creates fake idle tables and fake “who’s up?” confusion.

### 5) Use bracket and standings views as communication tools

Point players to the live bracket instead of answering the same question twenty times. If a TV / spectator board payload is available for your event, use it—public status reduces desk interruptions.

### 6) Admin only when needed

Reseeds, swaps, and score corrections should be rare, logged in your own notes if possible, and explained briefly to affected players. Heavy admin mid-event usually means the registration process was soft.

## Floor craft: directing like an adult

### Announce like a stage manager

Before match one, say the race, break rule, reporting method, and dispute path out loud. People listen better to a sixty-second speech than to a paragraph buried in a chat.

### Keep tables moving

Idle tables kill events. If two players are missing, call backups or adjust per your posted rules. The phone bracket should reflect reality quickly.

### Protect newcomers

Amateur nights die when regulars bully rules interpretations. Directors set culture. Be welcoming without being soft on posted standards.

### Separate money-set culture from tournament culture

Side bets happen in the real world. Do not let private side drama capture the event’s attention. The tournament’s contract with the field is the bracket and the posted rules. For private stake arrangements outside the event, players can use **[/play](/play)** another time.

## Seeding, skill talk, and fairness without false claims

Players often ask whether brackets should seed by APA skill level or Fargo-style ratings. Those systems are familiar reference points in American pool. Unless and until a product explicitly integrates an official data source, treat player-provided skill info as **contextual**, not as certified gospel. Do not market RackUp as an official APA or Fargo tournament authority.

Practical fairness options directors use:

- Random bracket for casual nights
- Light seeding based on known local strength when the room consents
- Separate flights if the skill spread is enormous

Transparency beats secretly protecting favorites.

## Spectator experience and hall energy

A readable bracket changes the social physics of a room. Friends can track a player’s path. Side tables stay engaged. Newcomers understand what they are watching. If your event supports a TV-oriented view, place it where it does not block traffic and where glare does not make standings unreadable. Digital clarity is part of hospitality.

## Post-event habits that grow the next field

1. Thank the hall and the players.  
2. Share next week’s start time the same night.  
3. Invite standouts to connect on RackUp for non-tournament play.  
4. Note format tweaks while memory is fresh (race too long? check-in too soft?).  
5. Encourage participants to keep practicing on **[/shots](/shots)** so the next event’s average quality rises.

Directors who only extract entry energy without community follow-up slowly lose fields.

## FAQ: pool tournament brackets on your phone

### Can I run a tournament with only my phone?

Many amateur events are phone-first now. A second device or a TV view helps for spectators, but the operational core is create → register → start → report → standings on **[/tournaments](/tournaments)**.

### Do players need accounts?

Expect participants to use the product’s normal auth flow for registration and reporting. Walk-up handling is a director skill—decide your policy before doors open.

### Is this a replacement for major league software?

Not necessarily. Use the right tool for season-long league administration versus a local night bracket. RackUp emphasizes network play plus tournaments together.

### How do stake matches relate to tournaments?

Different workflows. Private stake sets: **[/play](/play)**. Multi-player brackets: **[/tournaments](/tournaments)**.

### What if a score was reported wrong?

Use admin correction sparingly and communicate to both players. Prevention via immediate reporting is better.

### Can tournaments help me meet players if I am new?

Yes. Structured events are one of the fastest legitimate ways to enter a scene—then continue with **[/find](/find)** afterward.
